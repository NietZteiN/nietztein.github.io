Evidence

How it works. The text is split into paragraphs and sentences (markdown front matter, headings, links, code and HTML comments stripped), and each sentence is scored. Click any number on the set to see the sentences behind it; the advisories are real findings, not jokes, though they are also jokes.

Temperature
60 °F + 10 × Σ AFINN score ÷ (scored words + 3). AFINN scores run −5…+5; the +3 keeps one "wonderful" in a short post from declaring a heatwave. °C = (°F − 32) × 5⁄9.
Wind
mean words per sentence, read as mph; gusts = mean + 2σ. The wind rose counts the first word of each sentence by a closed-class list: pronoun, article, conjunction, preposition, adverb, or other (a content word).
Precipitation
marks per 100 words: commas = drizzle, semicolons = sleet, em-dashes = hail, exclamation marks = thunder, question marks = fog. Total inches = all marks per 100 words ÷ 10.
Humidity
function words ÷ all words (a 230-word closed-class list: pronouns, articles, prepositions, conjunctions, auxiliaries, common adverbs).
Pressure
1013 mb + 500 × (MATTR₁₀₀ − 0.72), where MATTR₁₀₀ is the type/token ratio averaged over a 100-word moving window. Richer vocabulary = high pressure = fair weather.
Visibility
10 mi × (2.2 − mean syllables per word), clamped to 0.25–10 mi. Syllables are counted by vowel groups with the usual silent-e and -ed fixes.
UV index
words in ALL CAPS or inside *italic* / **bold** markdown, per 100 words. 11+ is extreme.
Conditions
thunder if exclamation marks ≥ 0.8 per 100 words; fog if questions ≥ 1.6 per 100 words or visibility < 3 mi; snow if ≤ 32 °F and wet; rain if commas + 2 × (semicolons + dashes) ≥ 9 per 100 words; overcast if ≥ 6.5 or humidity ≥ 52%; partly cloudy if humidity ≥ 44% or below 60 °F; otherwise sunny. Fronts on the chart are jumps of ≥ 6 °F between consecutive paragraphs; isobars are marching squares over a Gaussian-smoothed field of the 3-paragraph moving average.

Sentiment lexicon: a 1,686-word single-token subset of AFINN-111 (Finn Årup Nielsen, 2011), Open Database License. Posts are read from this site's blog; pasted text stays in your browser. Any resemblance to an actual weather broadcast is the point.

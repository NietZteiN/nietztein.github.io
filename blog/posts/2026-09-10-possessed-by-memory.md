Three claims in there are worth separating out, because they usually come bundled and only two of them are right.

## The fertile wrong theory

Freud is wrong in most of the specific ways a theory can be wrong. The drive model is [hydraulics borrowed from the steam engine](#/post/metaphors-we-hallucinate-by), the case studies do not replicate, and the whole apparatus is unfalsifiable in the way Popper complained about. None of this is in dispute.

But wrongness is not the only axis. A theory can also be measured by what it makes possible to say. Freud opened a vocabulary — the unconscious, repression, transference, the idea that a symptom means something — and that vocabulary turned out to be enormously productive even where the mechanism behind it was fiction. Jung could build on it. So could Lacan, Klein, Winnicott. So could [Bloom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anxiety_of_Influence), who took Oedipal conflict and made it a theory of how poets read each other, which is probably the most durable thing anyone ever did with Freud.

The right way to read Freud is the way you read a research program that failed: not for the conclusions, but for the moves. He was willing to take a phenomenon nobody could measure and propose a structure for it anyway. That is a skill, and it is orthogonal to being correct.

## What behaviorism cost

The reaction to Freud went too far in the obvious direction. Skinner's response to unfalsifiable inner states was to declare that inner states are not the subject matter — only what can be operationalized, measured, reinforced. This was methodologically clean and substantively disastrous, because the thing you actually want to explain is mostly inside.

Linguistics is the clearest case. [Chomsky's 1959 review of _Verbal Behavior_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verbal_Behavior#Chomsky's_review) is the canonical demolition: the terms Skinner used — stimulus, response, reinforcement — either kept their laboratory meanings, in which case they plainly failed to describe language, or got stretched into metaphor, in which case they explained nothing. You cannot get productivity and structure dependence out of a reinforcement schedule. The field had to reintroduce the inner structure it had spent thirty years refusing to talk about.

The general failure mode: restricting yourself to what is currently measurable is not rigor, it is a bet that the measurable part is the important part. Often it is not. I think about this constantly with respect to interpretability, where we are extremely good at measuring the things that are easy to measure about models and much worse at asking whether those are the things that matter.

## Read widely

Which is why "read widely" is not a general-enrichment argument. It is specifically about where hypotheses come from.

Research has two phases and only one of them is taught. You are trained extensively in hypothesis _testing_ — statistics, experimental design, ablations, the whole apparatus of not fooling yourself. You are trained almost not at all in hypothesis _generation_, which gets treated as something that happens to you rather than something you do. But hypotheses do not appear from nowhere. They come from collision: a structure you know from one domain striking a problem in another and throwing off a spark. Eisenstein's [intellectual montage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_montage_theory), which I have [written about before](#/post/untitled) — two independent shots juxtaposed to produce a concept present in neither.

You cannot collide things you do not have. The size and heterogeneity of what you have read is not a nice-to-have; it is the raw material of the generative phase. A researcher who has read only their subfield can test beautifully and has nothing to test.

The related mistake is treating research reading and recreational reading as different activities. Reading papers because they are necessitated keeps you current. Reading poetry, philosophy, history — reading things with no plausible relevance to your work — is what gives you something to be current _with_.

## Possessed by memory

The last claim is the one I initially wanted to argue with and now think is the strongest: modern pedagogy undervalues memorization, and memorization is a precondition for thinking.

The case against rote learning was that it substitutes recall for understanding. Fair enough, as a critique of teaching multiplication tables and nothing else. But the conclusion that got drawn — facts are cheap now, look them up, teach critical thinking instead — mistakes where thinking happens. You cannot have an insight about material that is not in your head. Retrieval is not the same operation as having. A fact you can look up is available serially, one at a time, on request. A fact you have memorized is available in parallel, unbidden, ready to collide with whatever else is in there. The connection that constitutes an idea is made between things that are simultaneously present.

The chess evidence makes this concrete. [Chase and Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chunking_%28psychology%29) showed that masters do not have better memory in general — on random board positions they are no better than novices. What they have is tens of thousands of stored patterns, and those patterns are what perception runs on. The expert does not reason faster; the expert sees a different board. Memory is not a warehouse sitting next to cognition. It is the substrate cognition runs on.

"Possessed by memory" is [Harold Bloom's phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Bloom), and the possession runs the right direction — not you holding the material but the material holding you, surfacing unrequested, in the middle of a problem it has no business being relevant to. That is what a memorized poem does and a bookmarked poem does not.

## The obvious objection

The obvious objection, in 2026, is that language models have read more than any human ever will and memorized essentially all of it, and this has not made them good at generating hypotheses. True. I do not think it damages the argument so much as locate it.

Wide reading is necessary and not sufficient. What a model has is the corpus and the formal relations within it. What it lacks — and I have made [this argument at more length elsewhere](#/post/untitled) — is friction: material resistance, a stake in the outcome, the experience of a hypothesis failing on contact with something real. Reading gives you the material for collision. Something else has to want the collision to happen.

But that is a claim about what else is required, not a reason to skip the reading. The models are not bad at research because they read too much.
